*The rules of evidence for a measuring instrument, as settled by the Perpetual
Assize of Instruments, Gauges and Other Devices Which Purport To Know Things
(the Instrument Court), and adopted here in full, because in eleven thousand
years nobody has managed to improve on them and it seemed arrogant to try.*

*Instruments are required to be physically present for their own examination.
Over those eleven thousand years this has produced three courtrooms of increasing
size and one permanent hole in the side of a mountain, which is dealt with
below.*

---

## THE RULE FROM WHICH THE OTHERS DESCEND

> # An instrument that cannot be questioned is not evidence.
> # It is a rumour with a power supply.

Everything below descends from that one sentence. Not from "the instrument must
be correct", which is a hope, and which every instrument in this document was
sincerely believed to satisfy on the morning of the day it destroyed something.

**Correct is not a property an instrument can have on its own.** It is a verdict,
and verdicts require questioning, and questioning requires that the instrument
have made itself available for it: the working shown, the assumptions stated, the
source open, the log written, the reference named, and the whole apparatus
standing there prepared to be doubted by a stranger who owns better equipment.

sdrtop does not ask to be trusted. It asks to be checked. Those are opposite
requests and only one of them is ever honoured.

The Court's motto is carved above the entrance in a language nobody has spoken
for six thousand years. It is usually rendered ASK IT AGAIN. A determined
minority of scholars maintains that it reads ASK IT, AGAIN, and that the comma
changes everything, and the dispute has now outlived four of the buildings.

Both readings are instructions. Neither is a reassurance. In eleven thousand
years of jurisprudence, on four buildings, in a language that died, nobody has
ever carved TRUST IT over a door.

## PREFACE: In the Matter of an Instrument That Was Working Perfectly

For four hundred and six years, every instrument on Vesperid Nine agreed with
every other instrument on Vesperid Nine.

This was considered, at the time, the crowning achievement of the planet's
scientific establishment, and there were monuments. Three of them are still
standing, which is two more than the inquiry recommended. It was cited in
speeches. Schoolchildren were taught the date on which the last dissenting gauge
was brought into line, and the technician who did it had a modest but entirely
genuine public holiday named after him. It is still observed. It has been renamed
twice.

The dissenting gauge, it was established rather later, had been right from the
beginning. It is now in a museum, in a case, beside a small printed card that
does not mention this.

The instruments agreed because they were all wrong in the same direction, for the
same reason, about the same thing. Nobody uncovered this by noticing a
discrepancy, because there were no discrepancies to notice. No civilisation in
recorded history has ever been better protected against discrepancies than
Vesperid Nine was for four hundred and six years.

The period is remembered as **the Great Agreement**, which was what they called
it at the time as well, and which nobody has since found a way to say without a
short pause first.

During it, Vesperid Nine completed a canal, two orbital elevators and a full
planetary census, and all three of them worked, which is the part nobody enjoys
bringing up. A system that is wrong in one consistent direction is not a system
that falls over. It is a system that functions beautifully, for a very long time,
in a way that gets more expensive to correct every year that it does.

It came apart, in the end, because a visitor brought their own gauge. It had been
calibrated somewhere else entirely, by people with no opinion whatsoever about
Vesperid Nine, and it disagreed with absolutely everything.

The visitor was detained for eleven days.

The Assize then did the obvious thing and put the original instrument in the
dock. The proceedings were exhaustive, the public mood was not
generous, and the verdict was an acquittal that surprised absolutely everybody
except the three people who had read the transcripts, two of whom had been saying
so for some time and one of whom was the manufacturer and was therefore
discounted on principle.

The instrument had never malfunctioned. Not once, across four centuries, had it
reported anything other than exactly what it had measured, to a tolerance that
remains, frankly, a little embarrassing for the equipment that replaced it.

What it had measured was the width of the window it was looking through.

The word above the number said `BANDWIDTH`.

Nobody, in four hundred and six years, had ever asked the word anything at all.

---

None of that happened.

All of it happens continuously, in software, on a smaller scale, to fewer people,
with no monuments and no inquiry and no holiday: a quantity measured perfectly,
labelled as something it is not, agreeing beautifully with four other quantities
that are wrong for the same reason, and consulted with total confidence by
somebody with no way to check and no reason on earth to suspect they should.

sdrtop is one person's hobby project, built in evenings, on a desk, with two
radios on it. It has no Assize. It has one advantage over the scientific
establishment of Vesperid Nine, and it is the only advantage that has ever
mattered to anybody:

**It has agreed, in advance and in writing, to be cross-examined.**

That is the product. The spectrum is a feature. The waterfall is a feature. The
Friis noise figure model is a feature and a fairly nice one. *That* is the
product.

# THE RULES OF EVIDENCE

## RULE ONE: hearsay is not measurement 📄

A datasheet is a statement made outside this court, by a party with a commercial
interest in the outcome, offered to prove the truth of the very thing it asserts.

It is not admitted.

Not because it is false. Datasheets are usually rather good, written by people
who know the device intimately and would very much like you to succeed with it,
right up to the paragraph where they stop knowing and start hoping. That boundary
is never marked. It is not even always known to the author.

A datasheet has never once been wrong about a device. It has frequently been
wrong about *your* device, which is a different thing and is the one you have.

It is not admitted because it **cannot be asked a follow-up question**, and in
the entire history of measurement, everything that has ever mattered was in the
follow-up question.

> *The Assize admitted a datasheet exactly once, in the year 4,033, as an
> experiment. The device behaved precisely as described for the full duration of
> the hearing and then stopped, permanently, eleven minutes after the ruling. No
> cause was ever established. The ruling has never been reversed, on the grounds
> that reversing it would require admitting further evidence from the same
> source.*

**A radio gets a native backend once the radio has been on this desk.** Powered.
Tuned to something genuinely transmitting. Watched by a person in a position to
notice that something was subtly off, and permitted, at that moment, to be
disappointed about it.

Not once the documentation had been read closely. Not once the protocol was
understood well enough to implement with confidence. Not once a really rather
good model of the device existed in somebody's head. After it was **held**.

This rule is expensive and the expense is the point. It is why the supported list
is short, why it grows at the speed of a hobby budget rather than the speed of
enthusiasm, and why "planned" in this project means *needs hardware* and never
*needs an afternoon*.

Everyone who has ever written a backend for a device they did not own believed,
sincerely and quite often correctly, that they understood it well enough. That is
not the objection. The objection is that from the inside, understanding a device
and misunderstanding a device feel **identical**, and the instrument cannot settle
the question, because the instrument is the question.

Only the hardware can settle it. So the hardware settles it.

### ⟨ a note from the clerk, who was not asked ⟩

The hole in the mountain is Exhibit 7,314. It is a tide gauge.

It is four kilometres long, it was installed by a previous civilisation for
reasons still under polite dispute, and in the year 9,102 it was called to give
evidence in a disagreement about a harbour. Rule of procedure 4(a) requires an
instrument to be physically present for its own examination. The Assize
considered an exception, took eleven months over the question, and declined, on
the grounds that an exception to 4(a) would be an exception to 4(a).

So the court went to the tide gauge. The chamber was cut into the mountain around
it over the two years following, at a cost described at the time as obscene and
described now in tourist literature.

The gauge was found to be accurate in every particular. This is usually reported
as the embarrassing part. It is not the embarrassing part. The harbour dispute
was settled correctly, on evidence from an instrument that could not be brought
to court, questioned in the only place it could be questioned, and four
kilometres of excavated mountain is simply what a court looks like when it takes
its own rule seriously enough to be ruined by it.

Every generation since has proposed relaxing 4(a). The hole is the reason none of
them has.

**This project has no mountain. It has a desk, and it is the same rule.**

## RULE TWO: no witness may testify to what it did not observe 🚫

A witness who does not know is required to say so, in those words, and is then
excused with the court's thanks.

This is not a failure of the witness. It is the single most useful thing any
witness has ever done in this building, and it is genuinely remarkable how much
training it takes to get right.

The court has been misled a great many times. It has never once been misled by a
witness who said "I don't know." That is the only unbroken record in the
building, and it is held by the least impressive sentence in the language.

**A plausible number is not a slightly worse version of a true one.** It is a
different category of object, and it is the only category this court was ever
built to stop.

A plausible number does not arrive announcing itself. It arrives looking exactly
like the others, takes its seat among them, and is offered tea. By the time
anybody asks where it came from, four other numbers are citing it, and two of
them are in a report.

A missing reading is an inconvenience. The user goes and finds it elsewhere,
mildly irritated, entirely intact, their model of the world unharmed. A crash is
embarrassing and completely honest: it announces itself, it cannot be misread,
and nobody in the history of the trade has made a bad decision because a program
stopped.

A wrong number that looks right is neither of those. It is a trap that springs
later, somewhere else, on somebody who stopped wondering whether the instrument
might be lying, because it never had been.

So when sdrtop cannot work something out, that reading says so and stops. It does
not fall back to a reasonable default. It does not interpolate from a similar
device. It does not carry the last known value forward and hope nobody checks the
clock. It does not quietly round away an uncertainty because the panel looked
untidy carrying it.

> *The Signal bench once reported 7.49 MHz of occupied bandwidth for a clean
> broadcast station, and it reported it with total composure. It was measuring
> the span that happened to have been captured rather than the signal sitting
> inside it. That one number was feeding four others: the modulation badge
> guessed from a span, the adjacent-channel bands overlapped the very channel
> they were being compared against and read 0.0 dB on both sides with full red
> bars, and the verdict announced "adjacent splatter" for a signal that had none.*
>
> *Nothing crashed. Nothing was greyed out. Five readings agreed with one another
> perfectly, and all five were wrong, because all five were wrong about the same
> thing. It took a person sitting down and asking each number in turn whether it
> was actually true. Several were not.*
>
> *It is a small Great Agreement. They are all small until they are not.*

## RULE THREE: the payload is not the testimony 📦

"It said 51 bytes" is not testimony. It is a **summary** of testimony, prepared by
somebody else, for reasons of their own, and the court has learned to ask what
they left out on the grounds that it was not interesting. The parts people find
uninteresting are where this court does most of its work.

A courier is not an expert witness. A courier is somebody who was in the room and
can tell you the parcel was heavy. This is genuinely useful, and it is not the
same as knowing what was in it, why it weighed that, or whether it should have.

The question is **why 51**.

At what spreading factor, across what bandwidth, at what coding rate. How far
above the noise floor it arrived, and how much of that margin the preamble had
already spent before a single bit of payload was reached. How long it held the
channel, and what that cost everything else trying to use it. How many symbols
the decoder repaired on the way in, because a packet that arrived intact after
fighting for it and a packet that strolled in unopposed are the **same 51 bytes**
and are emphatically not the same event.

Whether the demodulator was confident or merely lucky, and how it can tell the
difference, which is a question most demodulators have never been asked and would
struggle with.

**A signal is not a container with a payload inside it.** A signal is a physical
event that happened in the world, and the payload is very nearly the least
interesting thing about it. Everything else is carried whether or not anybody
built a display for it. The only question ever genuinely on the table is whether
the instrument threw it away before you arrived.

sdrtop does not throw it away. That is the entire reason this program exists
rather than one of several perfectly adequate alternatives.

The FM bench is the precedent and it was built this way deliberately: deviation
measured about the carrier, the MPX baseband with its pilot and its stereo
injection percentage, CTCSS, AM depth with the positive and negative peaks kept
apart because they are different physics, RDS pulled off the subcarrier, accents
and all. It reports what a spectrum plot structurally cannot. It plays absolutely
nothing.

WiFi, Bluetooth, ADS-B, AIS, LoRa and DMR are on the roadmap. Each is held to
this or it does not ship, and it does not matter how long that takes. A packet
dump inside a nice border is not demodulation. It is a courier reading out
somebody else's envelope.

# THE MARKED EXCEPTION

*In which the court admits evidence it is not entitled to, says so on the record,
instructs the gallery precisely what it is worth, and turns out to have
strengthened the very rule it broke.*

### ⟨ a note from the clerk, on why this chapter is allowed to exist ⟩

The Assize's first act after the Great Agreement was the Absolute Rules Act,
which forbade any exception to any rule of evidence, on any ground, for any
reason, permanently. It was drafted in nine weeks by people who were not in the
mood, and it is still regarded as the most rigorous legal instrument the planet
has ever produced.

It held for four hundred years, a figure the Assize once quoted with some pride
and now tends to leave out.

The trouble was found by a clerk doing an inventory, which is how most things are
found. Of the four thousand or so rules then on the books, a great many had never
been invoked, never been argued against, and never been pressed on by anybody at
all.

**A rule that has never been pressed on gives back no information about itself.**
It cannot be told apart from a habit, and the archive was already full of habits.
Nobody could say which of the four thousand were load-bearing. Not because any of
them were bad. Because none of them had been tested, and an untested rule is not
a rule you have. It is a rule you are assuming.

So the Assize adopted the doctrine this chapter runs on, and it is narrower than
it sounds:

> **A rule may be broken once. Deliberately, in the open, with the boundary drawn
> and the breach marked on every document it touches.**

The breach is not a weakening. It is the only measurement of the rule anybody
will ever get. Everything outside the marked boundary is now *known* to be
governed rather than assumed to be, because the boundary is visible and a person
can walk around it and see where it stops.

The phrase they arrived at has been misquoted ever since, almost always by
somebody looking for a way out of something: **the exception proves the rule.**

Proves as in *tests*. As in a proving ground. As in proof spirit. Nobody on
Vesperid Nine has ever used it to mean *excuses*, and the Assize has fined people
for trying.

**The whole doctrine hangs on the marking.** An exception that is not marked is
not an exception at all. It is a rule that was quietly abandoned by somebody who
did not want the argument, and the archive holds several of those, which is
precisely how anyone knows the difference matters.

---

The Instrument Court holds that Rule One is not satisfied in respect of any radio
reached through **SoapySDR**. Airspy, the SDRplay RSP line, PlutoSDR, LimeSDR,
bladeRF, USRP, and whatever sits at the far end of SoapyRemote. None of those
devices has been on this desk. The backend was written from the API headers.

Under Rule One the evidence is inadmissible, and the court admits it anyway,
under a stated exception, with the following instruction, which is not optional
and does not get quieter further down the page:

**Every claim sdrtop makes about a radio it has never held is reasoned, not
verified. Those are different words for different things and this project will
not allow them to touch.**

The conditions of admission, all of them already met:

- **No device table exists in the source.** Frequency range, sample rates, gain
  range, the presence of an AGC, the presence of a baseband filter, the sample
  format and how many of its bits carry meaning: every one is asked of the driver
  at runtime. A device table would be testimony about hardware nobody here has
  held, which is Rule One wearing a false moustache and hoping.
- **Rule Two is untouched.** Where the driver cannot answer, the reading is
  absent. The RF bench's noise figure, MDS and linearity card does not appear at
  all on a SoapySDR device, because those model one specific front end stage by
  stage and SoapySDR does not publish a front end. Inventing that card would have
  taken an evening, it would have looked magnificent, and it would have been the
  worst thing in this program by a comfortable margin.
- **Another party's claim is entered as another party's claim.** `SoapyHackRF`
  announces a tuning range of 0 to 7.25 GHz where the datasheet says 1 MHz to
  6 GHz. sdrtop repeats the driver's number, the radio refuses the rest, and the
  log names the driver that said it. The court does not silently correct evidence
  it cannot verify. It also does not launder it.
- **The instruction is repeated wherever the evidence appears.** On the badge, in
  the status block, and on a hardware page carrying a table of what is actually
  proven that is not remotely flattering and is not going to be improved. A
  disclosure made once is a disclosure you are hoping nobody attends.

The exception stands until the evidence is replaced by the admissible kind. Every
one of those radios stays grey until somebody who owns one stands it on a desk
and files a report. Then that one, and only that one, is entered properly. The
debt is real, it is itemised, and it is repayable.

And here is the part that took a while to see.

**Before this breach, the First Law had never been tested.** Every radio sdrtop
supported was green, and "verified on hardware" was a phrase that applied to
everything the project shipped, which is another way of saying it distinguished
nothing at all. It was not a law. It was a habit that had never been inconvenient,
and it would have gone on being one indefinitely.

The grey badge is the first evidence that the green badge means something. There
was no way to obtain a grey badge without breaking something on purpose, in the
open, and writing down exactly where the break stops.

The boundary is drawn. The breach is marked on the badge, in the status block, in
the hardware table and in this document. And the First Law is stronger inside
that boundary than it ever was in the four hundred years nobody had a reason to
push against it.

The exception proves the rule. Tests it. Both.

## AFFIDAVIT

*Attached to the above at the request of the applicant, who asked to say this in
his own words rather than have the court say it for him.*

I broke my own rule. I want that written down by me, not paraphrased by an
institution I invented.

The rule was good. Hardware support lands only after physical testing, no
guessing from datasheets, and I meant every word of it. It is also why, every
single week, somebody asked whether this would talk to their Airspy and the
honest answer was no, and would remain no, because buying one of everything is
not a plan, it is a wish with a shipping address.

Here is the part that actually decided it. An instrument gets better in exactly
one way: somebody runs it on real hardware and comes back to tell me what it got
wrong. Under the rule as written, the only people on this planet able to do that
were HackRF and RTL-SDR owners. That is a very thin crew to carry a program whose
whole ambition is to be the most careful thing in your terminal.

Obeyed to the letter, my good rule would have kept this instrument small,
correct, unused and unimproved, permanently, and been right about it the entire
time. A rule that guarantees its own irrelevance has stopped protecting anything.
It has just become a thing I get to be right about.

So I broke it deliberately, in one place, and then spent considerably longer
making sure it could not spread than I spent writing the backend. Everything in
the ruling above is what that cost, and I would do it again.

It took me an embarrassingly long time to stop feeling bad about it. What finally
did it was working out that I had not weakened the rule. I had given it its first
edge. Before SoapySDR I could say "verified on hardware" about every single thing
in this program, which felt like rigour and was actually just a sentence with
nothing to push against.

I would rather have a rule with one marked scar on it than a rule nobody has ever
had a reason to test.

If you own one of those radios: **you are the visitor with their own gauge.** You
are the only thing that has ever ended a Great Agreement, and you will not be
detained for eleven days, because I have neither the authority nor the room.

An issue saying
"it works, here is what it reported" is worth as much to me as a crash report,
and arguably more, because a crash tells me where it broke and a working report
tells me where it held. There is no clever engineering substitute for a person, a
radio, and ten minutes.

*Signed, and entered into the record.*

# STANDING ORDERS

*The Rules, applied to the places they are most often lost.*

## I. All testimony is dated ⏱️

A witness who says "the situation is stable" is required to say when they last
looked.

The moment RX stops, every lab panel marks itself **[STALE]**. Not greyed out.
Not quietly frozen. Not left sitting there looking exactly as authoritative as it
did while it was true.

A number on a screen is a claim about **now**. The instant that stops being the
case and the number stays put, the screen has begun lying, and it lies in the
single most persuasive way available to any interface: by not changing. Nothing
draws less attention than a value holding perfectly steady. It reads as
stability. It reads as a well-behaved signal. It reads as good news.

A frozen reading offers nothing to doubt. That is precisely why it is labelled
instead.

> *The rule is named after a lighthouse keeper who testified, accurately and in
> remarkable detail, about a shoal. The shoal was correct. The keeper had last
> looked at it in a different decade, and had not thought to mention this,
> because nobody had asked, and nobody had asked because the testimony was so
> specific that asking felt rude.*
>
> *Every clerk is taught this case in their first week, and every clerk arrives
> at the same wrong conclusion first, which is that the keeper lied. The keeper
> did not lie. The keeper answered the question. The question was the problem,
> and the question was: is the shoal there.*

The only thing worse than no data is confidently wrong data.

## II. One witness, one account 🔁

No witness may give two different accounts of the same event in two different
parts of the room.

> *The ADC saturation reading used to disagree with itself. The Command Rail
> called it calm below 10 %, while the micro views and the lab benches went amber
> at 1 % and red at 5 %, so the same saturation could be green in one corner of
> the screen and red in another at the same instant, with nothing anywhere to say
> which was right. Both were defensible. That was the problem: a reader had no
> way to tell they were being told two things.*

There is one saturation scale now, and if SAT is red, it is red on every screen.

> *The Assize once took evidence from the same tide gauge in two chambers on the
> same afternoon and received two different answers, both of them correct. The
> chambers were reading two different faces of it. Each face carried its own
> painted scale, applied by different people, forty years apart, and both
> painters had been careful.*
>
> *That was the difficulty. If either of them had been careless it would have
> been found in a week.*

The same order binds every label. The adjacent-channel rows name the offset they
actually measured, so the label and the number can never drift apart. Where a
figure and its description are stored separately, they will eventually disagree,
and the disagreement will be discovered by somebody who trusted both.

## III. Exhibits are checked against the events, never against each other 📖

The documentation is not an accessory to sdrtop. It is where most people meet
this project, and it is held to the Rules exactly as the code is.

**Each fact lives in one place**, and the other pages point at it. The same thing
explained two different ways in two different files is not thoroughness. It is
two exhibits that have already begun drifting apart and have not yet been caught.
That is a documentation bug and it is worth an issue as much as a crash.

> *The docs were once rebuilt by checking every page against the code rather than
> against the other pages. It turned up fifteen places where the documentation
> described something the program does not do, and eight features that had
> shipped and were written down nowhere at all. Every one of those pages had been
> proofread. They had been proofread against each other.*

**Where the exhibits are unsure, they say so in the same font as everything
else.** Not in a footnote, not in smaller type, not phrased so a reader in a hurry
comes away encouraged.

### ⟨ concerning the archive, which is the Court's proudest possession ⟩

Every exhibit in the Assize archive is cross-referenced to the exhibits that
corroborate it. Pull any one of them and the card in the sleeve tells you which
others agree, in what respect, and on whose authority. It is a magnificent
system. Visiting archivists have wept.

It also means that when an exhibit is wrong, every exhibit pointing at it is
wrong, all of them agree, and the archive is at its most persuasive precisely
where it is least correct. A researcher checking a doubtful fact will find it
confirmed four times in under a minute, and will leave satisfied, and will be
carrying the error out of the building with them.

The Assize is aware of this. It has been aware of it for six thousand years. Two
official histories of the institution exist, and they disagree about almost
everything except the archive, which both describe as beyond reproach. Both
histories are held in the archive. Each is cross-referenced to the other.

The remedy, when it finally arrived, was not a better archive. It was a rule: an
exhibit is checked against the event it describes, and never against the exhibit
next to it, however tempting, however cheap, and however many of them there
are.

## IV. A safeguard that cannot fail is an ornament 🔍

If a check cannot be made to fail on demand, it is not a check. It is a comment
with a runtime cost and a reassuring tick mark in front of it.

> *The key overlay was once a hardcoded block of text. It claimed
> `[1] Preset: main` long after `[1]` had come to mean `command_rail`, and it
> shipped that way for several releases. Nothing connected the help to the
> dispatch, so nothing noticed, and every person who read that overlay was misled
> by a program that was otherwise working perfectly. It now reads the dispatch
> table as source text and refuses to let the two disagree. The help cannot drift
> any more, because drifting is a test failure rather than an embarrassment
> discovered by a stranger.*

This is why a reference generated from the code is admitted and a reference typed
out by hand is not, however carefully it was typed. Careful is a state of mind. It
does not survive contact with a Tuesday.

The same standard binds any check that merely appears to have run. A verification
step whose failure path continues anyway is worse than no verification at all,
because there is now a tick mark in front of the thing nobody checked, and it will
be believed by everybody, including the person who wrote it.

> *For six hundred years the archive was protected by a bell that would ring if
> the lower chambers flooded. It was tested every year, on a fixed date, by
> ringing it. The bell rang. The test passed. The result was minuted.*
>
> *Nothing in the arrangement had ever connected the bell to the water.*
>
> *The flood, when it came, was not especially bad. It is remembered at all
> because six hundred consecutive annual certificates were recovered from it, in
> excellent condition, each one confirming that the bell worked.*

## V. The court hears only what it can afford to summon 🪙

Every native backend requires the hardware to physically exist here. That is Rule
One, and Rule One costs money that a hobby does not have a great deal of.

This is stated plainly rather than dressed up as a roadmap, because a roadmap
implies a schedule and there is not one. There is a desk, a hobby budget, and a
list of radios that would each unlock a class of devices if one arrived.

No pressure attaches to that and none should. It is simply the honest mechanism,
written down, so nobody has to guess why their radio is not on the list and
nobody has to wonder whether asking twice would help.

Rule 4(a) has an unwritten companion that every clerk knows and no statute
contains: **the Court can only hear what it can get into the room.** This is not a
principle and nobody has ever been proud of it. It is a description of a
building.

Twice in eleven thousand years the Assize has attempted to summon a star. The
transcripts of both attempts are in the archive and are, by a comfortable margin,
the funniest documents on the planet.

This project has a desk. It is the same sentence, and it has been raised once or
twice as a limitation of the project. It is not a limitation of the project. It
is a desk.

## VI. The gallery is not an audience 👥

This is an open table, not a private garage.

An audience receives a finished thing and applauds or does not. A gallery reads
the transcript, argues with a measurement, files the issue that says "this number
disagrees with my bench equipment", and turns out to be right often enough to
change the code.

Rule Two makes this structural rather than sentimental. An instrument that
refuses to invent is an instrument that will **visibly** not know things, all over
its own interface, permanently. Those gaps are not failures to be papered over
before anybody notices. They are the invitation, and the most valuable ones are in
front of radios nobody here has ever held.

### ⟨ a further note from the clerk, still unasked ⟩

The Assize's own answer to the Great Agreement was the Second Opinion Act, which
required every measurement of consequence to be confirmed by a second instrument
of independent manufacture.

It worked extremely well for about nine years.

The difficulty was economic. Independent manufacture is expensive, and the market
did what markets do. Within a decade the two largest instrument makers on
Vesperid Nine were producing, between them, four brand names, eleven model lines
and one design. The Act was satisfied at every audit. Two instruments, two
manufacturers, two certificates, two differently coloured boxes, one set of
assumptions.

The Act was amended to require independent *design*. The manufacturers engaged
the same three consultants. The Act was amended to require independent
*consultants*. There were, by that point, three consultants.

The final amendment is still law. It gave up on defining independence
structurally and required instead that the second measurement be taken **by
somebody who does not care how it comes out**. This is much harder to define,
completely impossible to audit, and has never once failed.

**That person cannot be hired, legislated for, or manufactured. They can only
turn up.** Which is precisely what an issue from a stranger with their own radio
is, and why one of those is worth more here than any quantity of additional care
applied by me to a device I already own.

## VII. The court declines questions it lacks the apparatus to hear 📏

There is no audio. Not on any panel, not planned, and not because it was
difficult. The demodulator exists to measure a transmission, never to perform
one.

There is no transmit, on hardware perfectly capable of transmitting. Read only, on
purpose, permanently.

No mouse input is handled anywhere, and nothing in the program needs any.

**Every one of those is a ruling, and a ruling is not a missing feature.**

> *The Assize has been asked eleven times to rule on whether a thing was
> beautiful. It has declined eleven times, on the grounds that it holds no
> instrument for it and will not improvise one, and it has been criticised for
> this on all eleven occasions, in the press, at length, by people who would have
> been appalled if it had answered.*
>
> *The twelfth request is generally expected within the century. The Court's
> position is not under review.*

An instrument draws its authority from the size of the space around it. What it
declines to do is exactly what makes what it does do mean anything. A tool that
does everything has no opinion about what it is for, and a tool with no opinion
has never once had to choose between being useful and being right.

The same order binds what it *does* say. The dBm figures on the RF bench are
modelled and relative, and the panel says so on its own face, because a calibrated
reading requires calibrated hardware and this is emphatically not that. An honest
estimate, labelled an estimate, is a measurement. The identical number with the
label removed is perjury with better typesetting, and it is the easiest offence in
this entire field to commit by accident.

## VIII. The record must be readable, or it is not a record 📚

Accuracy is necessary. It has never once been sufficient.

A document that is entirely true and that nobody finishes has documented nothing.
It has arranged some facts in a location. A filing cabinet does that, and no
filing cabinet in the history of the world has explained anything to anybody at
two in the morning with a radio that will not start.

So the record here carries a story. `whats-new.md` is a history rather than a
list of dates. The README makes jokes. This document contains a planet, a
courtroom, a public holiday, a mistranslated motto and a visitor who was detained
for eleven days, and not one word of that is decoration. It is there because
somebody reading about the difference between *verified* and *reasoned* will
remember a lawsuit and will not remember a bullet point, and the difference
between those two words is the most important thing in this repository.

**The constraint is absolute, and it is the only reason the rest of this is
safe:**

> The story lives in the frame. It never touches the reading.

Be as funny as you like about the institution, the history, the desk, the budget
or the author, all of whom can take it. Never be funny about what a number means.
Never write a charming imprecise sentence where a dull exact one was available.
Where the two are in tension the exact one wins, every time, and the writer's job
is then to go away and find a way to have both, which is harder, and is the job.

The gradient runs the other way as the reader gets closer to the machinery. This
document is the most story-bearing thing in the repository. The README is
lighter. `user_docs` is lighter still and gets steadily plainer the nearer the
reader is to something not working, until `troubleshooting.md`, which is close to
humourless on purpose, because nobody arrives there in a good mood and nobody in
the entire history of the trade has ever wanted a joke from a page they opened
because their radio would not start.

Plain is not the same as dry. Not one page in this repository is permitted to be
dry. Dry is what happens when a writer stops imagining a reader, and a document
written for nobody has already stopped being true in the way that matters,
whatever its facts happen to be doing.

> *The debt this document owes to a particular Englishman with a towel is
> considerable, and is hereby entered into the record. He would have found the
> Assize's filing system unbearable, and he would have been right, and it would
> have taken him four hundred words, and it would have been the best part of the
> book.*

# 🕯️ THE REGISTER OF FALSE WITNESSES

*Testimony that has appeared credible in this building. Each has been heard here,
which is how it earned an entry.*

## The Witness Who Was Never Contradicted

Appears because the panel looked unfinished without one. Derived from a default, a
neighbouring value, or an assumption made months ago in an entirely different
context. Never marked as an estimate, because marking it would have drawn
attention to it, and drawing attention to it was the precise problem it was
invented to solve.

It is always the best-looking number on the screen. It has never once been
challenged, and it will tell you so, at length, and this is routinely mistaken
for an alibi. It is not an alibi. It is an attendance record.

**Excluded by:** Rule Two. Absent beats plausible, every time, with no tiebreaker
and no exception for how bad the gap looks.

## The Right Answer, Under Oath, For The Wrong Reason

The testimony is correct today. The reasoning underneath it is not. It passes
every test in the suite, because tests check answers, and its answers are fine.

It waits. It is in no hurry at all. It has, in the most literal sense available,
nothing better to do, and nothing in the test suite is looking for it, because
the test suite checks answers and its answers are impeccable.

> *The `[` and `]` gain keys were once hidden because the device was "single
> knob". The keys were hidden correctly and the reason was wrong: they are hidden
> because there is no second gain stage. Those two statements select exactly the
> same devices, right up until a SoapySDR radio arrives with one overall gain and
> no meaningful knob count at all, at which point a rule that had been correct for
> years begins producing wrong answers somewhere nobody thought to look, having
> given no warning of any kind, because there was nothing to warn about.*

**Excluded by:** writing down *why*, beside the code, at the time, in prose. Not
for elegance. Because the reason is the part that has to survive the next device
and the answer is not.

## The Witness Who Will Not Say Where They Were Standing

Reports in dB against nothing in particular. Or in dBm from an uncalibrated front
end with no note saying so. Perfectly precise, entirely unanchored. It will
eventually be compared against a figure from a different tool with a different
reference, and the comparison will be meaningless in a way neither number is
capable of admitting to.

Asked for a reference, it offers more decimal places.

**Excluded by:** naming the reference on the panel, and by giving the noise floor
as a density, so that the same receiver reads as the same receiver whatever the
sample rate.

## The Courier Called As An Expert

Decodes flawlessly. Produces the bytes. Can say nothing whatsoever about how they
got there, how close they came to not arriving, or what they cost the channel
around them. It is a protocol library with a UI bolted on, and it is a
demodulator in roughly the sense that a scale reading only "heavy" is a scale.

**Excluded by:** Rule Three, and by remembering that the payload was never the
interesting part.

## The Disclosure Made At Three In The Morning To An Empty Room

Technically entered into the record. Buried in a paragraph, on a page reached from
another page, in smaller type, in a sentence built so that a reader in a hurry
comes away encouraged. Every word of it true. Nobody who needed it received it.

It is in the record. So is everything else. That is what a record is.

This one is not a defect in the instrument. It is the Rule failing outside the
binary, which is the failure we are least equipped to notice in ourselves, because
from the inside it feels like modesty.

**Excluded by:** saying it on the badge, in the status block, and on the page,
until somebody complains that we keep saying it.

# THE STANDING ORDER

None of this is aspiration. There is no version of sdrtop that ships a number it
cannot defend under questioning, however good the release would have looked
carrying it.

If this program ever tells you something it does not actually know, that is not a
bug report. That is perjury, it breaks the only promise this project has ever
made, and it outranks every open issue, every roadmap item and every feature
anybody is waiting for.

Say so, loudly. You will be believed, and you will be thanked, and it will be
fixed before the interesting work resumes.
